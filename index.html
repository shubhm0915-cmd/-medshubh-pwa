💎 Nephrotic Syndrome

Understand the concept • recognise the clue • eliminate distractors • revise fast

FMGE PRIORITY • RENAL

Nephrotic Syndrome

Understand why protein leaks first. Then use the clinical clue and biopsy pattern to identify the most likely lesion.

≥3.5 g/dayAdult proteinuria
EdemaKey clinical clue
PLA2RMembranous clue
V34 • START HERE

🧠 Core concept snapshot

Start with the defining concept, mechanism and clinical clues. Open the deeper explanation when you need more clarity.

🔴 MUST KNOW

  • Nephrotic syndrome: heavy proteinuria (adult ≥3.5 g/day), hypoalbuminaemia and oedema.
  • Child + selective proteinuria + normal LM / foot-process effacement → think MCD.
  • Nephrotic = protein loss; nephritic = haematuria / RBC casts / inflammatory picture.

🟡 FREQUENTLY TESTED

  • MCD: normal LM, negative IF, diffuse foot-process effacement on EM.
  • FSGS: HIV / heroin / obesity; focal and segmental sclerosis.
  • Membranous: anti-PLA2R, subepithelial deposits, spike-and-dome.
  • MPGN pattern: double-contour / tram-track GBM.

🔵 EXTRA UNDERSTANDING

  • Underfill and overfill both contribute to oedema.
  • Urinary antithrombin loss contributes to thrombosis risk.
  • Biopsy and immunotherapy decisions depend on age, cause and clinical context.
Question-solving pathway: First identify the syndrome → use age / clinical association → read LM, IF and EM clues → choose the lesion → check secondary causes before selecting treatment.
Rapid recall: MCD = foot-process effacement • FSGS = focal + segmental scar • Membranous = subepithelial + PLA2R • MPGN = tram-track • Nephrotic complication = renal vein thrombosis.
CONCEPTUAL THINKING • HOW TO REACH THE ANSWER

🧠 Apply the concept — don't just recall a fact

1. Identify the patternWhat syndrome is described? Here: heavy proteinuria + hypoalbuminaemia + oedema.
2. Find the decisive clueAge, associated disease/drug, or the specific LM / IF / EM finding.
3. Link clue to mechanismPodocyte injury, immune deposits, or GBM remodelling — which mechanism best fits?
4. Eliminate distractorsReject options that do not match the strongest clue; don't choose from a generic association alone.
5. Check the question askedDiagnosis, investigation, complication, or next step? Answer the actual task, not just the disease name.
Worked clue: Child + nephrotic presentation + normal light microscopy + diffuse foot-process effacement on EM → Minimal change disease. The EM clue is the discriminator; “normal LM” does not mean normal glomerular filtration barrier.

01

Overview & diagnostic framework

First understand what defines the syndrome

Nephrotic syndrome is a clinical syndrome caused by increased glomerular filtration-barrier permeability to plasma proteins, producing heavy proteinuria with downstream systemic effects.

Adult criteria
  • Proteinuria ≥3.5 g/24 h (or nephrotic-range spot protein/creatinine ratio).
  • Hypoalbuminemia, commonly <3.0 g/dL (severity varies).
  • Edema, often with hyperlipidemia and lipiduria.
Children
  • Nephrotic-range proteinuria: commonly ≥40 mg/m²/hour or urine protein/creatinine ratio ≥2 mg/mg.
  • Minimal change disease is the commonest primary cause in young children.
  • Initial biopsy is not routinely needed in a typical steroid-sensitive presentation.
Core concept: The syndrome is not a single histological diagnosis. It is a clinical pattern caused by several glomerular diseases.
02

Edema: two complementary mechanisms
  • Underfill: hypoalbuminemia → reduced oncotic pressure → fluid shifts to interstitium → reduced effective circulating volume → RAAS/ADH activation and sodium retention.
  • Overfill: primary renal sodium retention (including collecting-duct sodium reabsorption) expands extracellular volume and contributes to edema.

Exam trap: edema is not explained by hypoalbuminemia alone; renal sodium retention also matters.

03

Etiology & classification

Separate primary glomerular disease from systemic causes
Primary glomerular causes
  • Minimal change disease (MCD)
  • Focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS)
  • Membranous nephropathy
  • Membranoproliferative pattern / C3 glomerulopathy
Secondary causes
  • Metabolic: diabetes mellitus, amyloidosis.
  • Autoimmune: SLE (class V membranous pattern).
  • Infections: HBV, HCV, HIV, malaria (context dependent).
  • Drugs: NSAIDs; selected other agents.
  • Malignancy: solid tumours; Hodgkin lymphoma (classically MCD association).
Age-oriented clue: Child with typical nephrotic syndrome → think MCD first. Adult with new nephrotic syndrome → evaluate secondary causes and consider biopsy according to clinical context.
04

Clinical presentation

Recognise the syndrome before naming the lesion
Symptoms & signs
  • Periorbital edema (often noticed in the morning), dependent pitting edema.
  • Weight gain, frothy urine, ascites, pleural effusion.
  • Severe edema may cause scrotal/labial swelling or anasarca.
  • Blood pressure may be normal or elevated depending on cause and volume status.
Urine & laboratory clues
  • Heavy proteinuria, often albumin-predominant.
  • Oval fat bodies / fatty casts; Maltese-cross pattern under polarized light.
  • Hypoalbuminemia, hyperlipidemia; lipiduria.
  • Microscopic hematuria or reduced complement suggests an alternative/mixed process in the right context.
Clinical distinction: Nephrotic = heavy protein loss + edema. Nephritic = inflammatory glomerular injury with hematuria, RBC casts, hypertension and reduced GFR. Overlap can occur.

MCD — remember

Podocyte injury; LM looks normal, but EM reveals diffuse foot-process effacement. Often steroid responsive in children.

FSGS — remember

“Focal” = some glomeruli; “segmental” = part of an affected glomerular tuft. More likely to progress and may recur after transplantation.

Membranous — remember

Subepithelial immune deposits; anti-PLA2R supports primary disease. Nephrotic patients have important thrombotic risk.

MPGN — remember

Double-contour / tram-track appearance reflects mesangial interposition and GBM remodelling; often presents with nephritic-nephrotic features.

06

Diagnostic approach & investigations

Confirm → quantify → assess severity → identify cause
  1. Confirm proteinuria: urine dipstick and microscopy; quantify with spot urine protein/creatinine ratio or 24-hour urine protein when needed.
  2. Assess severity: serum albumin, creatinine/eGFR, electrolytes, lipid profile, BP, weight and volume status.
  3. Search for secondary causes when indicated: glucose/HbA1c, ANA and complement, HBV/HCV/HIV testing, serum/urine electrophoresis, anti-PLA2R and age-appropriate malignancy evaluation.
  4. Decide on renal biopsy: commonly considered in adults with unexplained nephrotic syndrome, atypical features or when histology changes treatment. Typical childhood steroid-sensitive presentation often starts without biopsy.
Red flags / atypical features

Persistent hematuria, low complement, significant hypertension, rapidly falling GFR, systemic inflammatory features, older age with atypical course or steroid resistance → broaden differential and seek nephrology assessment.

07

Management — supportive care + cause-specific treatment

Treat the patient, control proteinuria, then treat the lesion
Supportive measures
  • Dietary sodium restriction; fluid restriction only when clinically indicated.
  • Loop diuretic for troublesome edema; assess intravascular volume carefully and avoid overly rapid diuresis.
  • ACE inhibitor / ARB when appropriate to reduce proteinuria and control BP; monitor creatinine and potassium.
  • Vaccination, infection prevention and prompt treatment of infection.
  • Manage dyslipidemia according to persistence and overall cardiovascular risk.
Cause-specific principles
  • MCD: corticosteroids are commonly first-line for idiopathic childhood disease.
  • FSGS: distinguish primary from secondary/adaptive forms; immunosuppression is not appropriate for every FSGS patient.
  • Membranous: risk-stratified approach; supportive care and selected immunotherapy under nephrology guidance.
  • Secondary disease: treat the underlying cause (e.g. diabetes, infection, autoimmune disease or offending drug).
Anticoagulation trap: nephrotic syndrome increases thrombosis risk, but prophylactic anticoagulation is not automatic for everyone. Individualise based on albumin, disease, bleeding risk and clinical context.

Common traps
  • Nephrotic syndrome is a clinical syndrome, not synonymous with MCD.
  • Normal LM does not mean normal kidney: EM may show the diagnostic podocyte lesion.
  • FSGS describes a pattern of injury; identify whether primary, genetic or secondary/adaptive.
  • MPGN is a pattern, not one single disease entity.
  • Do not label every nephrotic patient as needing steroids or anticoagulation.

💎 Normal Pregnancy & Antenatal Care

Pearl 01 • Build a safe pregnancy pathway from booking to birth

Normal pregnancy & ANC

Pregnancy care is a sequence: confirm and date pregnancy → identify maternal/fetal risk → screen treatable disease → monitor growth and wellbeing → plan safe delivery.

280 daysFrom LMP (40 weeks)
11–13+6 wkNT window
18–22 wkAnomaly scan window
V42 • OBG QUESTION-FIRST 01

🧠 Core concept snapshot

Antenatal care is not just a list of visits. Every contact should answer: Is the mother well? Is the fetus growing appropriately? Is there a complication that can be detected early and acted upon?

🔴 MUST KNOW

  • EDD by Naegele rule: LMP + 9 months + 7 days (for a regular 28-day cycle); adjust for cycle length when appropriate.
  • First booking should be early, ideally in the first trimester; document LMP, dating certainty, obstetric history, medical/surgical history, medicines and risk factors.
  • Routine baseline assessment: BP, weight/BMI, blood group & Rh, CBC, urine assessment, and locally recommended infection screening.
  • Folic acid 400 micrograms daily before conception and through early pregnancy for average-risk neural-tube-defect prevention; high-risk dosing is clinician-directed.
  • Ultrasound confirms location, viability, number, dating and later anatomy/growth; do not use ultrasound alone to replace clinical assessment.

🟡 FREQUENTLY TESTED

  • NT scan window: 11 to 13 weeks + 6 days (CRL-based first-trimester screening window).
  • Detailed fetal anomaly scan: usually 18–22 weeks.
  • Gestational diabetes screening is commonly performed at 24–28 weeks; earlier testing may be indicated for risk factors or pre-existing diabetes suspicion.
  • Anti-D immunoglobulin is offered to eligible unsensitised RhD-negative pregnant patients according to local protocol, and after sensitising events.
  • WHO recommends 8 antenatal contacts; India's national programme has its own scheduled ANC/PMSMA service framework. Do not mix the two schedules in an exam answer.

🔵 EXTRA UNDERSTANDING

  • Fundal height becomes a useful serial screening measure after about 20 weeks; a discrepancy prompts assessment, not an automatic diagnosis.
  • Low-risk pregnancy does not need routine serial growth scans or umbilical artery Doppler without an indication.
  • Dating by first-trimester CRL is generally more accurate than later biometric dating when LMP is uncertain.
01

Confirm pregnancy, date it correctly

Dating errors affect every later decision
Pregnancy confirmation
  • Urine/serum β-hCG detects trophoblastic hCG; serum becomes positive earlier and is more sensitive.
  • Transvaginal ultrasound can identify an intrauterine gestational sac earlier than transabdominal ultrasound.
  • A single hCG value cannot reliably determine pregnancy location or viability; interpret with symptoms, serial trend and ultrasound.
Gestational age & EDD
  • Gestational age is counted from the first day of LMP, not from conception; it is approximately 2 weeks more than embryonic age in a typical cycle.
  • Naegele: LMP + 9 months + 7 days (28-day cycle).
  • Use early ultrasound/CRL when LMP is unknown, cycles are irregular or dates conflict significantly.
Exam trap: A pregnancy of 8 weeks by LMP is approximately 6 weeks from fertilisation in a typical 28-day cycle. Do not subtract two weeks when reporting obstetric gestational age.

Visit schedule: avoid a common exam mix-up
  • WHO model: 8 antenatal contacts — first by 12 weeks, then around 20, 26, 30, 34, 36, 38 and 40 weeks.
  • India: follow current national/state ANC schedule and service delivery guidance; PMSMA provides assured antenatal check-up services on the 9th of each month for eligible pregnant women, especially in the 2nd/3rd trimester.
  • For clinical care, visit frequency is individualised by risk; complications need more frequent review.

04

Radiology & obstetric ultrasound integration

Know what each scan can answer — and what it cannot
First trimester
  • Confirm intrauterine location, viability, number and chorionicity when possible.
  • CRL is the preferred early ultrasound parameter for dating.
  • NT is measured in the validated 11–13+6-week window and interpreted with the appropriate screening pathway; it is not a standalone diagnostic test.
  • Adnexa and free fluid matter when ectopic pregnancy is in the differential.
Second trimester
  • Anomaly scan evaluates fetal anatomy, placenta, amniotic fluid and cervix as appropriate.
  • Placental location is documented; a low-lying placenta in mid-pregnancy may move away from the internal os as the uterus grows.
  • Soft markers modify aneuploidy risk but must be interpreted in the full screening context.
Third trimester
  • Biometry (BPD, HC, AC, FL) estimates fetal size; abdominal circumference is especially useful in growth assessment.
  • Amniotic fluid: deepest vertical pocket or AFI, according to local protocol.
  • Umbilical artery Doppler is used in suspected placental insufficiency/FGR, not as routine screening in every low-risk pregnancy.
Safety & limitations
  • Ultrasound uses sound waves, not ionising radiation.
  • Use a clinically justified scan and trained interpretation; a normal scan does not exclude every congenital or genetic condition.
  • MRI may be used for selected maternal/fetal questions when ultrasound is insufficient; gadolinium is generally avoided unless essential.
05

Prevention, supplements & immunisation

Know the purpose; check local schedule for exact products and timing
Nutrition & supplements
  • Folic acid: 400 μg/day for average risk; prior NTD-affected pregnancy or selected high-risk states need higher prescription dosing.
  • Iron–folic acid: provide according to national antenatal supplementation programme; treat documented anaemia with therapeutic regimen.
  • Calcium supplementation is recommended in populations with low dietary calcium intake to reduce pre-eclampsia risk; follow national protocol.
  • Balanced diet, appropriate weight gain, physical activity and avoidance of tobacco/alcohol are core advice.
Vaccination
  • In India, Td-containing vaccine is used in pregnancy as per national immunisation schedule; check previous immunisation status and current national guidance.
  • Influenza, COVID-19 and other vaccines depend on current national guidance, risk and local policy.
  • Live vaccines such as MMR and varicella are generally contraindicated during pregnancy; administer postpartum if non-immune and indicated.
Medication rule: never assume a drug is safe because it is commonly used outside pregnancy. Check gestational age, indication, dose and a current pregnancy-specific reference.

07

Clinical reasoning & common exam traps

Apply the timeline rather than memorising disconnected dates
  • Uncertain LMP + early pregnancy: use first-trimester CRL dating rather than guessing from fundal height.
  • NT measured at 15 weeks: outside the validated NT screening window; do not interpret it as a standard NT screen.
  • Normal anomaly scan: reassuring anatomy assessment, but it does not rule out all chromosomal, functional or later-developing disorders.
  • Low-risk pregnancy + normal growth: do not order routine Doppler merely because it is available.
  • RhD-negative, unsensitised patient: assess antibody status and follow anti-D prophylaxis protocol; sensitised patients require specialist management, not routine prophylaxis alone.
